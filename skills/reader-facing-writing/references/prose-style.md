# Keep the prose plain

Read this while drafting or tightening sentences, once the reader, the structure, and
what each section must carry are already settled. These rules govern wording, not what
the document contains — the skeletons in the main skill and `shaping-checklist.md` say what must be
there, and none of them license saying it twice.

- Use active voice, specific verbs, short sentences with one idea each, plain words over impressive ones, and no marketing filler.
- Default to the shortest version that carries the point: every sentence must add a new fact, decision, or consequence, and a sentence that restates, merely announces, or elaborates what a neighbor already established is cost without content (the licensed previews are the restatements other rules in this skill mandate, its reference files included: the first-screen layer, an agenda, a leading takeaway at any level, a visual's takeaway title, the one-line conclusion citing a demoted layer). Short is a limit on wording, not on substance: cut restatement and padding, never the context, mechanism, or caveat the reader needs to understand and act without coming back to ask. When a load-bearing sentence runs long, tighten its wording rather than dropping it.
- If one sentence says it clearly, one sentence is all it gets: do not inflate a point into a paragraph, or wrap a short answer in headings, background, and summary scaffolding to look substantial. Keep structure proportional to content — a section that exists to fill out a template is padding, and a short document that answers the question beats a long one that performs thoroughness.
- Cut connective padding as a class: throat-clearing openings, transitions that only announce structure ("notably", "in summary", "as mentioned above"), closings that repeat what the section just said, and nominalized phrasing ("perform an analysis of" for "analyze"). Structure and headings already tell the reader where they are; padding only delays the content.
- In Chinese output, put the concrete subject and action before long modifiers; replace weak-verb-plus-abstract-noun constructions, stacked de-modifiers, filler function words, and unnecessary passives with the direct verb.
- Let each Chinese sentence pick up a person, object, action, or result the previous one introduced. Drop connectors when order and causality already carry the relation, and repeat the exact term when synonym rotation would blur identity.
- Cut every line that serves the author rather than the reader: slogans, disclaimers and scope hedges ("this is not a commitment"), stance assertions ("we believe", "trust us"), boasts about your own restraint ("no hype here, just facts"), rally lines aimed at the audience, and your own housekeeping decisions (where a file lives, what is version-controlled). The discriminator: a boundary statement about the evidence itself ("this is a prototype", "a human still approves each step") is content; a statement about how you are talking is filler, however true it is. Keep any disclaimer the user or a compliance rule mandates.
- On a medium with a separate notes channel — a deck's speaker notes, a doc's margin — keep your own reasoning, caliber hedges ("do not overstate this"), and presenter navigation ("next slide") in that channel, not on the audience-facing surface, which carries only what the reader takes away. Your analysis of the material is not part of the material; sweep such leaked meta-commentary as a class, not one line at a time.
- On a persuasion or advocacy piece, do not pour cold water: a true but deflating point still belongs, but state it and reframe it as the next opportunity, and move the defensive detail to the notes as rebuttal ammunition — change the emphasis, never suppress the truth.
- Calibrate terms to the reader: define what they won't know, don't explain terms they already use, and use each term consistently. Establish a name before it carries weight as evidence (the first-appearance one-liner in `shaping-checklist.md`) — an unestablished name lands as a bare assertion however authoritative it is to you. The opposite failure costs as much: when something reads as unclear to a domain audience, the fix is almost always an ambiguous figure's basis or an undefined internal codename, not the vocabulary — translating terms they use daily into plain speech reads as condescension.
- Put the literal mechanism next to every analogy, never in place of it — if the reader can ask "but what actually happens, and why does that follow?" and the text has no answer, the analogy is concealing a gap instead of closing one.
- For a senior or expert audience, write each claim as a plain declarative judgment; colloquial metaphors, punning compressions, and rallying slogans read as lightweight and get cut — most of all in titles, which are judged hardest.
- Decompress claims instead of telegraphing them: shorthand that packs a claim, its evidence, and its point into one fragment forces the reader to rebuild the logic and loses even an expert audience — state each as a self-contained sentence that lands without prior context. Decompressing completes the logic, never licenses padding: the result is more short sentences, not longer ones.
- Keep each example or aside no heavier than the point it makes, and give each claim one well-chosen example — add a second only when it covers a case the first cannot reach; stacked examples of the same case are length without added evidence.
- Choose examples that are current, meaningful to this audience, and consistent with the claim they support — drop stale tools, insider-only cases, and any example that undercuts its own point.
- Do not narrate the document itself — the licensed self-references are those other rules in this skill mandate, its reference files included (the first screen's map of where details live, the opening agenda, leading takeaways, a member file's place in its set's map, named cross-references); cut "this document will" and "as we saw above".
